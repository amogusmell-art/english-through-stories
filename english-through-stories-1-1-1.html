Movie

Forrest Gump

A slow, folksy narration and simple sentence structure make this one of the most approachable classics for English learners.

About

The film follows an ordinary man with a low IQ who, through kindness and persistence, finds himself present at major moments in American history. What makes it useful for learners is its narration: short, plain sentences, an even pace, and a narrator who often explains things simply, as if to a listener hearing the story for the first time.

Key vocabulary

childhood sweetheartsomeone you loved since you were a child"She was his childhood sweetheart, and he never stopped loving her."
shrimp boata small fishing boat used to catch shrimp"They bought a shrimp boat and started a small business."
veteransomeone who has served in the armed forces"Many veterans found it hard to adjust to civilian life."
stock marketthe system where shares of companies are bought and sold"He invested his savings in the stock market almost by accident."
to run cross-countryto run long distances across open land, not on a track"He decided to run cross-country, for no particular reason at all."
destinythe events that will necessarily happen to someone in the future"He often wondered whether people control their own destiny."

Practice dialogue

Ray: Why do you run every single morning, rain or shine?

Tom: I don't know. I just started one day, and I kept going.

Ray: Didn't you ever want to stop?

Tom: Sometimes. But then I figured, I'd already come this far.

Ray: That's a strange way to make a decision.

Tom: Maybe. My mama always said, you never really know what's coming next.

An original practice dialogue written in the calm, plain-spoken style of the film — not a transcript from the movie.

Useful phrases

against all odds— despite very low chances of success
come what may— whatever happens, no matter the outcome
to turn a corner— to reach a point where things start improving
one thing led to another— used to describe a chain of events without explaining every step
to keep going— to continue despite difficulty
Movie

Inception

A dream-heist thriller that mixes technical planning vocabulary with abstract talk about ideas and memory.

About

A team of specialists enters people's dreams to extract — or plant — ideas. The film moves between corporate boardrooms and dream worlds that bend architecture and physics, which gives learners exposure to both formal, businesslike English and more abstract, philosophical language about the mind.

Key vocabulary

subconsciousthe part of the mind we're not actively aware of"The idea needs to feel like it came from her own subconscious."
extractionthe act of taking something out, here, an idea from someone's mind"The extraction has to happen before he wakes up."
blueprinta detailed technical plan or design"She drew up the blueprint for the dream's architecture."
to plant an ideato make someone believe an idea is their own"It's much harder to plant an idea than to steal one."
totema small personal object used to check what's real"He always carried his totem, just in case."
labyrintha complicated network of paths, a maze"The lower levels of the mind can feel like a labyrinth."

Practice dialogue

Mara: How do we know this idea is really his, and not something we planted?

Dev: We don't. Not for certain. That's the risk.

Mara: If it goes wrong, we can't just wake him up.

Dev: Then we make sure it doesn't go wrong.

Mara: You always say that like it's simple.

Dev: It never is. That's why we plan for every level.

An original practice dialogue in the film's tense, technical register — not a transcript from the movie.

Useful phrases

to lose track of— to no longer know or remember something
on the same page— in agreement, understanding each other
to buy into an idea— to start believing in or accepting an idea
a leap of faith— to trust something without proof it will work
to cut it close— to have very little time or margin for error
Movie

The Pursuit of Happyness

A biographical drama about perseverance, built on clear, everyday English about work, money, and family.

About

A struggling salesman takes an unpaid internship at a brokerage firm while raising his young son and facing homelessness. The dialogue stays grounded and practical throughout, which makes it a good source of everyday vocabulary around job hunting, finances, and parenting under pressure.

Key vocabulary

internshipa temporary position, often unpaid, taken to gain work experience"Only one intern out of twenty would get the job."
unpaidnot receiving money for work done"He took the unpaid position hoping it would lead somewhere."
evictionbeing forced to leave a place you're renting"They faced eviction after falling behind on rent."
résuméa document summarizing your work experience and skills"He rewrote his résumé the night before the interview."
stockbrokersomeone who buys and sells shares for clients"Becoming a stockbroker required passing a difficult exam."
perseverancecontinued effort despite difficulty

Practice dialogue

Manager: You do realize this internship doesn't pay anything?

Chris: I understand. I still want the chance.

Manager: Most people quit after the first month.

Chris: I'm not most people. I need this to work out.

Manager: Fair enough. Be here at seven tomorrow.

An original practice dialogue inspired by the film's tone — not a transcript from the movie.

Useful phrases

to make ends meet— to have just enough money to cover basic needs
to fall behind— to stop keeping up with payments, work, or a schedule
to land a job— to successfully get a job
against the odds— despite a low chance of success
to work things out— to find a solution to a difficult situation
Series

Friends

Widely used to teach conversational English — banter, sarcasm, and the rhythm of everyday speech.

About

The sitcom follows six friends navigating jobs, relationships, and daily life in New York, mostly across a shared apartment and a coffee shop. Because so much of the show is dialogue-driven and set in ordinary situations, it's one of the most commonly recommended shows for practicing natural, spoken English.

Key vocabulary

roommatesomeone you share a home with"Finding a good roommate can be harder than finding an apartment."
breakupthe end of a romantic relationship"She was still upset about the breakup a week later."
to hang outto spend casual time with someone"Do you want to hang out this weekend?"
awkwarduncomfortable or embarrassing"The silence at dinner was incredibly awkward."
sarcasmsaying the opposite of what you mean, usually to joke or mock"You could hear the sarcasm in his voice."
banterplayful, teasing conversation between people who know each other well

Practice dialogue

Jamie: So how was the date?

Alex: Oh, it was great, if you enjoy talking about someone's ex for two hours.

Jamie: That bad?

Alex: I've had more fun waiting for the bus.

Jamie: Well, at least you got a free dinner out of it.

Alex: There's always a silver lining.

An original practice dialogue in the show's casual, joking style — not a transcript from the series.

Useful phrases

no big deal— not something to worry about
long story short— to summarize something quickly
I'm in— used to agree to join a plan
a silver lining— a positive side to a bad situation
to be over someone— to no longer have feelings for a former partner
Series

Sherlock

Fast, articulate British English built around deduction, evidence, and precise vocabulary.

About

A modern retelling of the classic detective, solving cases through careful observation and logical reasoning. The show is dense with precise, formal vocabulary, delivered quickly — good practice for learners ready to move past basic conversational English toward more sophisticated, structured speech.

Key vocabulary

deductiona conclusion reached through logical reasoning"His deduction was based on a single detail everyone else missed."
suspecta person believed to be responsible for a crime"They had three suspects but no solid evidence."
crime scenethe location where a crime took place"Nothing at the crime scene had been touched."
alibiproof that someone was elsewhere when a crime occurred"His alibi checked out, so they moved on to the next suspect."
cluea piece of evidence that helps solve a mystery
forensicsscientific methods used to investigate crimes

Practice dialogue

Watts: How could you possibly know he'd been travelling?

Holt: The mud on his shoes. It's only found near the docks.

Watts: That could mean anything.

Holt: Not combined with the receipt in his pocket, dated yesterday.

Watts: You read his pocket?

Holt: I read everything. That's rather the point.

An original practice dialogue in the show's precise, deductive style — not a transcript from the series.

Useful phrases

to put two and two together— to draw an obvious conclusion from available facts
case closed— the matter is settled, no more discussion needed
to read between the lines— to understand a hidden meaning
to check out— (of a fact or alibi) to be confirmed as true
rather the point— exactly the reason or purpose of something
Series

Stranger Things

1980s small-town America and a supernatural mystery, told through the casual speech of a group of kids.

About

In a quiet American town, a group of kids investigates strange government experiments and a growing supernatural threat. The show blends the informal, playful speech of its young characters with the tenser, more adult language of the adults around them — useful for hearing both registers side by side.

Key vocabulary

supernaturalsomething that cannot be explained by science"Nobody believed the supernatural explanation at first."
conspiracya secret plan by a group, often involving deception"They suspected a government conspiracy behind the disappearances."
walkie-talkiea small portable two-way radio"They stayed in touch using walkie-talkies."
arcadea place with coin-operated video games"They spent every Friday at the arcade."
curfewa set time by which someone must be home"Her curfew was nine, and she was already late."
small towna place where everyone tends to know each other

Practice dialogue

Robin: You hear anything on the walkie-talkie?

Dustin: Just static. Nothing since eight.

Robin: That's not exactly reassuring.

Dustin: I know. But if something happened, we'd know, right?

Robin: I hope so. My curfew was an hour ago.

Dustin: Yeah, mine too. Let's give it five more minutes.

An original practice dialogue in the show's casual tone — not a transcript from the series.

Useful phrases

to freak out— to become very worried or upset
no way— expresses disbelief or refusal
to keep an eye on— to watch or monitor something
something's off— something seems wrong or unusual
let's give it a shot— let's try it
Game

Life is Strange

A narrative choice-driven game about teenage life, journaling, and the weight of small decisions.

About

A teenage photography student discovers she can rewind time and uses this ability while navigating friendship, choices, and a small-town mystery in the Pacific Northwest. The game's script leans heavily on natural teenage speech and internal narration, making it good practice for informal, expressive English.

Key vocabulary

flashbacka scene depicting an earlier event"The flashback explained why she never trusted him again."
to rewindto go backward in time or in a recording"She could rewind a few minutes to undo a mistake."
consequencea result of an earlier action or decision"Every choice had a consequence she couldn't always predict."
journala personal notebook for recording thoughts and events"She wrote in her journal almost every night."
small-towntypical of a place where few things stay secret
photographythe art or practice of taking photographs

Practice dialogue

Max: If I could take it back, I would.

Chloe: You can't rewind everything, you know.

Max: I know. I just wish I did.

Chloe: Some things you just have to live with.

Max: That's the part I'm still figuring out.

An original practice dialogue in the game's reflective, informal tone — not a transcript from the game.

Useful phrases

to take something back— to undo or retract something said or done
to live with something— to accept and continue despite a difficult outcome
to figure something out— to understand or solve something over time
it's not that simple— used when a situation has more complexity than it seems
no matter what— regardless of the circumstances
Game

Portal 2

A puzzle game narrated by a dry, sarcastic AI — great for picking up irony, understatement, and technical phrasing.

About

Trapped in an abandoned testing facility, the player solves physics-based puzzles while a sarcastic artificial intelligence provides a near-constant stream of commentary. The writing is precise and often ironic, which makes it useful for learners who already have solid basics and want to pick up on tone, dry humor, and technical vocabulary.

Key vocabulary

test chambera room designed for running a controlled experiment"The next test chamber requires two portals instead of one."
artificial intelligencea computer system able to perform tasks that normally require human thought"The artificial intelligence running the facility had its own agenda."
sarcasmsaying something ironic, often to mock or joke"It was hard to tell when the sarcasm ended and the threat began."
protocola fixed set of rules or procedures"Standard protocol required a full facility reset."
gravitythe force that pulls objects toward the ground
momentumthe force an object has because of its motion

Practice dialogue

System: Congratulations. You've completed the chamber in record time.

Subject: Thanks. Was that meant to be a compliment?

System: It was a fact. I don't do compliments.

Subject: Could've fooled me.

System: Please proceed to the next chamber. Try not to disappoint me twice.

An original practice dialogue in the game's dry, sarcastic style — not a transcript from the game.

Useful phrases

could've fooled me— used sarcastically to say something wasn't obvious at all
to proceed with something— to continue or move forward with an action
for the record— used to state a fact clearly, often for later reference
to take it personally— to feel offended by something not necessarily meant that way
easier said than done— something that sounds simple but is hard to actually do
Game

The Last of Us Part II

A post-apocalyptic survival drama with mature themes — vocabulary around loss, loyalty, and survival.

About

Set years after a fungal outbreak has devastated society, the story follows characters driven by grief and a desire for revenge across a dangerous, overgrown America. The writing is heavier and more emotional than the other titles here, offering vocabulary around difficult, adult themes alongside survival and combat terms.

Key vocabulary

survivalthe state of continuing to live despite danger"Survival meant trusting almost no one."
infectedcarrying or affected by a disease"The infected had taken over most of the city."
revengean action taken to punish someone for a past wrong"Revenge was the only thing keeping her going."
wastelanda place that has been destroyed or abandoned"The city had become an overgrown wasteland."
loyaltystrong, continued support for someone
griefdeep sorrow, especially after a loss

Practice dialogue

Ellie: We could still turn back. Nobody would know.

Dina: I'd know. And so would you.

Ellie: Maybe that's enough reason to stop.

Dina: Is it? After everything we lost?

Ellie: I don't know anymore. I just know we can't stay here.

An original practice dialogue in the game's tense, emotional tone — not a transcript from the game.

Useful phrases

to turn back— to stop and return the way you came
to hold a grudge— to continue feeling angry about something in the past
to let go— to stop holding on to anger, pain, or the past
there's no turning back— a decision or action can no longer be reversed
to watch someone's back— to protect or look out for someone
